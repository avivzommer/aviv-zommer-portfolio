The problem

The first trial in a company built on sales

SysAid is an enterprise ITSM platform that has grown entirely through direct sales for its whole history. Every new customer started with a sales conversation, a demo, and a procurement cycle. The product had never had to make a first impression on its own.

License Manager Advanced is a module within SysAid that discovers SaaS applications running across an organization and surfaces cost savings. The business wanted to introduce a PLS motion as a first step toward full PLG: a free 14-day trial that lets prospects evaluate the product autonomously, get to value on their own terms, and then make a qualified, informed call to sales.

I led design across four parallel workstreams, and the core tension in all four was the same: the product's value is discovery. Finding SaaS applications your organization didn't know it was running. The trial had to demonstrate that value clearly without giving it away fully. Show enough to create urgency; not so much that there's no reason to upgrade.

The harder part of the job wasn't the UI. Sales needed to believe the trial wouldn't bypass them: it would generate better-qualified leads, not self-serve conversions that skipped the conversation entirely. Aligning marketing on who the trial was targeting, and what message prospects would arrive with, was a major workstream alongside the product design itself.

The approach

What I could learn, and what I decided

Traditional user research needs access to users before they experience the product. For a trial, that's impossible: the trial IS the first experience. So I researched from the two directions that were open to me. Before launch, I looked outward at how other products solve the same problem. After launch, I watched what real trial users actually did.

Before launch

Competitor analysis

With no trial of our own to learn from, I went through how others do it: ITSM competitors such as Freshworks, monday.com and Jira, and SaaS products outside our category that are known for their onboarding.

  • Onboarding has to be step by step. The good ones never show the whole setup at once. They ask for one thing, confirm it, then ask for the next.
  • The trial is sold inside the product. In-product messaging and marketing work together to push people toward the trial, so the paywall is one moment in a sequence, not the whole pitch.
After launch

Session recordings

I used Datadog session recordings and events to watch real trial users and see where they dropped out of the flow. Two things stood out.

  • On the paywall, the call to action wasn't clear. People looked at the page and didn't know where to click. This led to paywall version 2
  • Onboarding was the biggest drop-off. People clicked "Start free trial" and then didn't know what to do or how to set things up. This led to the guided setup
Throughout

Deciding together with sales and marketing

The trial only works if sales trusts it, so the evidence was shared. We kept one dashboard in Salesforce and Mixpanel and brought three sources to the go-to-market meetings: feedback from the field through customer success, the funnel data, and the recordings. The decision to run another iteration was made in that room, together.

The pre-trial paywall, designed twice

The paywall is the first thing a prospect sees and the one screen every trial has to pass through. I designed a first version in the month before launch. Then the recordings showed people looking at it without knowing where to click, and customer success was hearing the same from the field. I redesigned it to convert better: the second version says less, shows more, and fits on one screen. Drag across the frame below to compare them.

Drag the handle to compare. Scroll inside Version 1, hover Version 2.
Version 1 · What I learned

A landing page inside the product: a headline, two calls to action, then section after section of value in one long scroll.

  • Thorough is not the same as convincing. The strongest proof sat far below the button asking for the commitment.
  • The call to action got lost. In the recordings, people looked at the page and didn't know where to click. "Start trial now" and "Contact us" sat side by side with equal weight.
  • It described the product instead of showing it. Prospects had to read about savings and take my word for them.
Version 2 · What I learned

Rebuilt around one constraint: no scrolling. One headline, one action, and the product itself in the middle. It is live, so hover the applications. Open it full size

  • Show the value, don't describe it. A row that reveals $91,200 in potential savings when you touch it needs no paragraph to back it up.
  • A constraint is a prioritization tool. One screen forced a question for every element: does this earn its place before the click?
  • Two sources beat one. The recordings showed where people got stuck and customer success explained why. Together they were the brief for this version.

Onboarding, from a checklist to a guided setup

Starting the trial is only worth something once real data shows up, and that depends on connecting a discovery source. The first onboarding was a checklist with a single path, and the recordings showed it was the biggest drop-off in the whole flow: people started the trial and then didn't know what to do. I redesigned it as a short guided setup that goes one step at a time, with a choice of method and a clear picture of what happens next. The full flow covers 25 screens, including every error and timeout state. These three are the happy path.

The design

A full-page checklist that replaced the Applications page until setup was finished. Three steps, Microsoft Entra ID only, and a separate "Start the discovery process" button at the end. One path, and nothing else to see until it was done.

What I learned
  • A step that is already done is noise. "Enable License Manager Advanced" arrived ticked. It made a two-step task read like three, plus one more button to actually begin.
  • One path means one way to get stuck. Entra ID was the only option, so anyone who couldn't complete it had nowhere else to go.
  • Blocking the page hides the product. The checklist stood in place of the applications table, so people set things up without seeing what they were setting up for.

1 · Choose a method. Two ways to discover applications, each with what it requires stated before the user commits. "Set up later" keeps the product open.

2 · Two steps, tracked in place. Each step ticks off when it is complete, so the user always knows where they are, even after leaving to grant a permission in Microsoft.

3 · What happens next. Connection confirmed, with honest timing: data within an hour, refreshed daily. Then an invitation to widen coverage with the second method.

The design

A split modal over the real Applications page. Pick a method, follow two steps, and see a preview of the result beside them the whole way. Setup became something you can start, pause, and finish.

What I learned
  • Say what it takes before asking for it. Putting each method's requirement on the first screen moves the bad surprise to the moment it costs nothing.
  • Keep the reward in view. A preview of the applications table sits beside every step, so the effort of connecting always has its payoff next to it.
  • Finishing setup is not the finish line. Discovery takes time, so the last screen sets expectations for the wait instead of leaving an empty table to explain itself.

The in-trial experience

The core design decision of the entire project. Inside the trial, users see their real applications table from day one: total savings, utilization rates, newly discovered apps. A quiet banner at the top counts down the 14 days. The trial shows the top 20 applications, and the product has usually found far more.

We knew from the start where the limit should appear: at the bottom of the table. It borrows a pattern people already know from consumer apps. You scroll, you reach the end, you want to keep going, and that is the moment you are asked. No popup, no interruption. By then the user has seen the value and is looking for more, and the way to get it is a conversation with sales.

The in-trial state. A full applications table with estimated savings, utilization rates, and discovery data visible from day one. The 14-day countdown banner at the top maintains gentle urgency without blocking the experience.

The scroll reveal. At the bottom of the table, after the user has explored their 20 visible apps, the full count appears: "Uncover all 241 apps?" with one button, Contact us. The placement is intentional. Earn the user's attention first, then show them what they're missing.

In-product guidance

Using UserFlow to drive adoption

Getting users to connect their environment is step one. Getting them to enrich the data (adding license counts, cost information, and reviewing discovered apps) is what actually makes the product valuable during the trial window. We used UserFlow to bridge that gap without requiring a support rep or onboarding call.

The design logic: the more data a user puts into License Manager during the trial, the more accurate their savings estimate, and the stronger the upgrade case. UserFlow was the mechanism for nudging users toward that enrichment at the right moment, with the right framing, without interrupting the core experience.

The walkthrough trigger. After the scan completes, UserFlow surfaces a tooltip showing utilization data and estimated savings already available. The ask is low-friction: a 1-minute walkthrough, with an easy dismiss if the user prefers to explore on their own.

The guided setup panel. A right-side panel walks through enrichment steps in order: connect Entra ID, add total licenses, add total cost, create custom applications. Contextual tooltips point to the exact column or field in the table, so users never lose their place in the product.

Trial end and expiry experience

When the 14-day trial ends, the user sees a single page: "Your free trial is complete. Upgrade to License Manager Advanced for full access to: Manage your 241 discovered applications. Track usage and reclaim licenses. Ensure license compliance. Detect Shadow IT." Then: "Ready to continue?" with a "Request pricing" button. Below that, a quiet link to share trial feedback. The design challenge was making this feel like a natural handoff, not a wall. The specific number (241 applications) is pulled from the user's own scan, so it's personal. The list of locked features is exactly what they explored during the trial. The ask is clear but not coercive, because the user already knows what they're missing.

The trial end page. The user's own discovery results (241 applications) are surfaced in the upgrade ask. The list of locked features maps directly to what they explored. "Request pricing" is the only hard CTA; a feedback link gives users a low-friction exit that still generates signal.

Looking back

What I took from it

01

Launch early, then watch

There were no users to interview, so the first version was the research. Shipping in a month and watching recordings taught me more than another month of polish would have.

02

A trial is won or lost in onboarding

Getting someone to click "Start free trial" is the easy part. The biggest drop-off came right after it, and that is where the redesign effort paid back most.

03

Shared evidence builds trust

Sales, marketing and product looked at the same dashboard and the same recordings. Decisions about the next iteration were made together, so nobody had to be convinced afterwards.

What's next

People start the trial, reach their data and ask to talk to sales. The step that still lags is turning those requests into booked meetings, and that is the focus of the next iteration.
