SysAid

Rebuilt SysAid's form builder, designed inside the product's own code

IT admins use templates to define what every incident and request form asks. The old builder couldn't express conditional logic and sent admins to three places to create one field. I redesigned it end to end, working directly in a copy of the real product instead of drawing it first.

The problem

Thirteen screens to build one form, and no way to make it smart

Every ticket in SysAid starts from a template: the form that decides what an incident or a request asks. To see how far the builder had fallen behind, I did the job myself. I took one realistic task, an employee onboarding request form, and built it in the live product as an IT administrator would, capturing every screen on the way.

Job 1 · Get to a form I can shape

The field palette only lists fields that already exist

There are 287 templates and no way to create a missing field from the builder. The most consequential choice, the record type, is made without being told what it changes.

Job 2 · Get my questions into the system

The database is the interface

Creating a field means leaving for the legacy admin, where 114 custom fields are listed by database name. Five of the eight inputs are technical. Saving doesn't create the field; the admin waits and refreshes a status column.

Job 3 · Ask only what is relevant

It cannot be done

A field has four switches: Required, Hidden caption, Hint, View only. No conditions, no dependent lists, no visibility by role. The requirement isn't worked around. It is abandoned.

Job 4 · Put it in front of employees

Publishing is somewhere else

Two checkboxes read like publishing and configure nothing. The real work is a second form designer in the legacy admin, where a field is hidden by moving every other field into a "Visible" list.

The approach

What fifteen other products do, and where logic should live

I mapped each of the thirteen steps against how other products handle it: the closest ITSM competitor, Freshservice, plus ServiceNow, Jira, Monday, Typeform, Google Forms, Jotform and others. Fifteen in all, most of them walked live.

One pattern held across every one of them: everyone else treats the form as the unit of work. SysAid treats the database column as the unit of work, and makes the admin do the join. Creating a field in place, writing logic in the builder, and previewing as the audience are not differentiators any more. They are the baseline, and they set the four parts of the redesign.

The open question

Three places a rule could be written

The benchmark resolved into three archetypes. They trade off against each other: the more a design shows the whole chain of dependencies, the more it costs in space and effort.

Option A

A tab on the field

Works: no navigation, the admin has already selected the field. Hurts: one field's rules at a time, so answering "what depends on Position?" takes many clicks.

Option B

A strip on the canvas

Works: the whole chain is visible without clicking anything. Hurts: the canvas is the scarcest space there is, and a template with fifteen conditional fields stops reading as a layout.

Option C

Its own workspace

Works: every rule in one list beside a dependency map. Hurts: the least discoverable, and rules are written away from the fields they affect.

The recommendation

Ship A, borrow the canvas chip from B, and hold C until templates get long. Normally that recommendation would have gone into a deck. This time I could do something better with it: build all three and compare them on the real screen.

The method

I designed it inside the product, not next to it

This project was one of the first built in the prototype machine, an environment our team made for designing in code. It is a frozen copy of the real product's frontend, running on a fake backend. A designer describes a change to an AI coding agent, and the agent builds it as a layer on top of the product, using only components from Glow, our design system.

I own the Glow library, and I designed how the machine should work: what it includes, what it refuses to do, and how a designer moves through it. Our design technologist did most of the technical build, and we worked on its design together.

My layer

The feature

The new template builder. One folder that overlays the product at runtime. Glow components only: no hand-made buttons, no colours outside the tokens.

Frozen

The product, as it ships

A pixel-identical copy of the real frontend. Nobody edits it, so every prototype stays comparable to the real thing. One switch flips between the shipped product and my design, on the same screen.

Fake

Realistic data with no real backend behind it. Nothing a designer does here can reach a customer.

What it changed

Three things I couldn't have done in a design file

01

Decide on the running screen

The three logic options weren't argued from wireframes. Each one ran with real data and real field counts, and the decision was made by using them.

02

Find the design system's real gaps

Building with actual components showed what Glow was missing: no radio group, no data table, a chip that can't be a trigger. Each gap was proven and logged instead of worked around.

03

Hand over working states, not pictures

Empty, loading, error and conflict states exist because the prototype needs them to run. Engineering starts from code built on the product's own components, so the path to production is weeks instead of months, with no handoff redesign.

01Fields 02Logic 03AI 04Publish

Create a field without leaving the builder

In the current product, a missing field ends the session. The admin leaves the builder, defines a database column in the legacy admin, waits for it to exist, adds its values on a third page, and only then comes back. In the new builder a field is made where it is needed, in the language of the question.

New fields are dragged in from tiles, and you can drag one. Watch a Select field being dropped into the form, then press Reset and try it: drag the Select tile over the form, and a dashed slot shows where it will land while the other fields move aside. Drop it, name it, type its values. Clicking a tile adds the field at the end.

Interactive recreation, simplified for this page. It is hand-built for the case study to match the prototype, and contains no product code.

The canvas shows, the panel sets. Clicking a field only ever selects it. Its name, behaviour, default value and who can see it live in one panel, and quick actions (settings, AI, move, duplicate, delete) hang off the field itself.

What it replaces

Eight inputs to create one field, five of them technical ("Compatibility Mode", "DB Field Name"), then a silent wait for the server to provision a column, then a separate page with 135 lists in one unsearchable dropdown to add its values.

Decisions
  • Making and reusing are different acts. New-field tiles sit above the catalogue of existing fields, so each has its own place.
  • Values belong to the field. They are edited in the panel as a plain list, not on a settings page with a Key / Caption grid.
  • One kind of click. An earlier version let controls on the canvas take input. It made the design surface behave like a form to fill in, so I removed it.
01Fields 02Logic 03AI 04Publish

Logic that reads as a sentence

This was the blocker. Today a field has four switches and nothing else, so "show Machine only for the positions that need one" cannot be expressed at all. The new builder gives every field a Logic tab with two things: rules for when the field appears or is required, and filters for which values it offers.

A rule is a sentence, and you can write one. Watch the rule being added, then try it: select a field, press Add rule, and complete "Show and require Licence key when Licence already owned is Yes". The chip on the canvas marks every field that carries logic.

Interactive recreation, simplified for this page. It is hand-built for the case study to match the prototype, and contains no product code.

Filters use the same grammar. "Offer MacBook Air and PC when Position is Product, Sales or Support." Position decides the machine, the machine decides its memory and whether a display choice exists at all.

How it changed

The first editor read "When … Then …" with two checkboxes. After comparing it with Monday's forms I moved the action to the front, because the admin arrives with the outcome in mind. The option filter started as a grid of every value against every answer; in use it was replaced by sentences, and the grid was removed.

Decisions
  • Three options were built, not drawn. A tab on the field, a strip on the canvas, and a separate rules workspace all ran in the prototype behind a switch. The tab won; the canvas chip was borrowed from the strip.
  • Our word is not the author's word. Nothing on screen says "cascade". It says "Offer … when …" and "Decides these fields".
  • Fields every ticket carries have no logic. Priority and Status are on every ticket whatever the template says, so a template cannot make them decide anything.

Start from a sentence, set up rules in chat

Most admins know what the form is for before they know which controls build it. So creating a template starts with a prompt, and anything in the builder that says AI opens the same AI Chatbot the rest of the product uses.

Creating a template starts with a sentence. Pick the Software installation card and it fills the box instead of sending it, because the detail that makes a template useful is the part only the author knows. Press Create template: the AI Chatbot opens and says what it is building, and the form arrives one field at a time so it can be read as it lands.

Interactive recreation, simplified for this page. It is hand-built for the case study to match the prototype, and contains no product code.

The assistant acts on one field. It opens already knowing which field it is about, offers four things it can do, and asks one question at a time. The rule it writes lands in the Logic tab with a short glow, in the same place a hand-written rule would.

How it changed

The first version of "create rules with AI" was a dedicated panel with its own suggestions, preview and Apply button. It lasted an hour. One AI entry point should look and behave like the other, so the panel went and the work moved into the chat.

Decisions
  • Twelve suggestion chips became four. One per kind of logic, each starting a short guided conversation.
  • A chat request is applied, not proposed. The admin asked for something and should see it done, with Edit and Remove one click away.
  • Honest about the prototype. The chat here is scripted. The design record says what is real and what still needs the model behind it.
01Fields 02Logic 03AI 04Publish

Preview it as the audience, publish it safely

In the current product, publishing is two checkboxes that configure nothing, followed by a second form designer in the legacy admin. The new builder keeps it in one place: see the form as each audience will, get told about broken logic when it breaks, and publish as a job you can walk away from.

Preview runs the form, so try it. Answer "Licence already owned". Yes brings in the licence key and requires it. No asks for a business justification instead. Nobody is asked for both. The switch is named by where the form is seen, Portal or Ticket, not by who is looking.

The same form, as the ticket. The header band with priority, category and assignment stays where it is on a real ticket, and the admin-only Direct manager field appears.

Publishing is one decision, and a job you can walk away from. Press Publish changes, choose who gets it, and publish. The window closes at once, the header shows the status, and the canvas stays usable while the job runs.

What it replaces

A portal form designer that opens in a separate window, picks from 141 templates in a dropdown with no search, and hides a field by moving every other field into a "Visible" list.

Decisions
  • Conflicts are caught where they are made. Delete a field a rule depends on and a toast says so, with Go to rule and Undo. The canvas chip turns to "Rule conflict", and the publish modal lists what must be fixed first.
  • No progress bar. A progress bar for a backend job is usually a lie. A status and a toast say what is happening and roughly how long.
  • The draft saves itself. There is no "Save as draft", because the builder is the draft.

Looking back

What I took from it

01

Do the user's job before redesigning it

Walking the thirteen screens myself gave the project its brief. Every design decision afterwards could be traced to a numbered step it removed.

02

A layout can't be judged until it holds real data

Many choices that looked right as a description were reversed the first time they ran with real field names and real lengths. Working in code made that cost minutes instead of a review cycle.

03

The AI builds, the designer still decides

The agent wrote the code. Every call about what stays on screen, what a control is named and which option wins was mine, and each one is recorded with its reason.

What's next

Handing the builder to engineering, putting it in front of admins to test the logic editor with their own forms, and connecting the chat to a real model in place of the scripted one.
